How occupational therapy helps your child

Occupational therapy supports children with developmental delay, autism, ADHD, sensory processing differences, coordination difficulties, and feeding challenges — as well as children without any diagnosis whose everyday skills need a boost. Every plan begins with a thorough assessment and is built around your child's strengths.

Sensory integration therapy

Understanding how your child processes sound, touch, and movement — and building routines that help them stay calm, organized, and ready to learn.

Autism support

Individualized, respectful therapy that builds daily-living skills, regulation, and participation — at your child's pace, around your child's strengths.

ADHD & attention

Practical strategies for focus, organization, and self-regulation that carry over from the therapy room to the classroom and home.

Fine motor skills & handwriting

Developing the hand skills behind drawing, cutting, buttoning, and confident, legible handwriting.

Gross motor & coordination

Strengthening balance, posture, and body awareness so your child can play, sit to learn, and keep up with peers.

Feeding therapy

Moving from stressful mealtimes and very limited diets toward comfortable, varied, family-friendly eating.

School readiness & developmental delay

Building the attention, independence, and foundation skills children need to start school successfully — and catching up when milestones lag.

Parent & school coaching

Clear, practical strategies for home and classroom — because most progress happens between sessions.

Frequently asked questions

What is pediatric occupational therapy?

Pediatric occupational therapy helps children build the skills they need for everyday activities — playing, learning, self-care, and taking part at school and home. Therapists work on areas such as fine motor skills, sensory processing, feeding, attention, and school readiness.

How do I know if my child needs an OT assessment?

Consider an assessment if everyday activities consistently cause frustration or avoidance out of proportion to your child's age — strong reactions to sounds or textures, difficulty with drawing or dressing, a very limited diet, or teachers reporting your child struggles to sit and attend. You do not need a diagnosis to ask an occupational therapist a question.

At what age can a child start occupational therapy?

There is no minimum age — occupational therapists work with infants through adolescents, and earlier support is generally better.
