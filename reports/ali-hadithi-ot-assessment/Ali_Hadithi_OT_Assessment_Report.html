Section 01

Reason for Referral

Ali Hadithi, a 3-year-2-month-old boy, was referred for an Occupational Therapy assessment. His caregivers raised concerns about sensory sensitivities, a short attention span, motor coordination difficulties, and inconsistent eye contact during daily routines and play.

The assessment aims to:

  • Look at Ali's current sensory, fine motor, and gross motor skills.
  • Identify what helps and what gets in the way of his play, self-care, and early learning.
  • Understand how sensory processing affects his attention and regulation.
  • Set clear, personalised therapy goals.
  • Give caregivers, and nursery staff where relevant, practical guidance to support his development at home and at school.
Section 02

Assessment Methods

These findings are based on clinical observation, informal assessment tools, and conversations with Ali's caregivers across several sessions:

  • Clinical observation of sensory and motor performance
  • Structured play-based assessment
  • Sensory processing observation (proprioceptive, vestibular, visual, tactile, and auditory)
  • Fine and gross motor skills observation
  • Muscle tone and strength assessment
  • Balance and postural control assessment
  • Activities of daily living (ADL) observation
  • Caregiver interview and developmental history review
Section 03

Behavioural Observation

Ali was active and energetic throughout the assessment. He moved often between activities and showed genuine curiosity about his surroundings.

  • His attention was shorter during less preferred or table-top tasks.
  • He engaged and cooperated noticeably more when an activity motivated him.
  • He needed occasional redirection to stay with a task, especially longer ones.
  • He was comfortable with hands-on guidance from the therapist.

When something interested him, Ali's participation and cooperation improved considerably — a promising sign for building attention through activities he enjoys.

Section 04

Interactive Skills

Ali's social engagement is developing but not yet consistent.

  • His eye contact was weak and often hard to maintain, with both the therapist and his caregivers.
  • He engaged more easily during activities he found highly enjoyable or sensory-rich.
  • Joint attention and back-and-forth interaction appeared, but didn't last long.
  • He responded to his name inconsistently, especially when absorbed in his own play.

With structured practice, particularly around eye contact and shared attention, these interactive skills are expected to strengthen.

Section 05

Relationship to Objects

Ali shows real curiosity when exploring toys and familiar objects.

  • He explores objects by sight and touch, though this is sometimes brief and driven more by sensory interest than a clear goal.
  • Purposeful play — stacking, building, sorting — is emerging but not yet consistent.
  • He's drawn to toys with strong sensory feedback: textured, moving, or noisy ones.
  • Reduced arm strength and a shorter attention span make more demanding hands-on tasks harder to sustain.
Section 06

Body Awareness

Ali's sense of his own body and how it moves is still developing.

  • Reduced awareness of his body's position during movement, such as going from the floor to standing.
  • Difficulty copying simple body movements or postures on request.
  • Difficulty planning and carrying out new or unfamiliar movement sequences.
  • Relies heavily on what he can see and touch to know where his body is during play.
Section 07

Sensory Processing

Ali has sensory processing difficulties across several areas, which affect his attention, regulation, and participation in daily and play-based activities.

Proprioceptive

Trouble sensing his body's position and judging how much force to use; seeks out strong movement and pressure to feel regulated.

Vestibular

Under-responds to movement, so he seeks out extra motion — this also affects his balance and stability.

Visual

Finds it hard to keep his visual attention on a task or pick out relevant details.

Tactile

Reacts differently to different textures — sometimes seeking touch, sometimes avoiding it — which shapes which play materials he'll try.

Auditory

Responds inconsistently to voices and sounds, especially when other sensory input is competing for his attention.

Overall Impact

Together, these difficulties make it harder for Ali to stay attentive, regulated, and engaged — especially in busy or unfamiliar settings.

Clinical Note Ali responds well to structured sensory input — proprioceptive and vestibular activities in particular — to help him regulate and stay ready to learn.
Section 08

Muscle Strength and Tone

Ali shows reduced muscle strength, especially in his arms, along with muscle tone that is functional but not always well controlled.

  • Noticeable arm weakness during weight-bearing, pushing, and resisted movements.
  • Reduced core strength, which tires him during longer floor or seated play.
  • Reduced grip strength, which limits how long he can manipulate objects.
  • Muscle tone is adequate but doesn't yet fully support steady posture during movement.
Section 09

Gross Motor Skills

Ali's gross motor coordination is developing but still immature for his age.

  • Follows 1–2 step gross motor instructions ("jump, then clap") with a visual or verbal cue.
  • Runs, jumps, and climbs, but without much control or refinement.
  • Struggles with longer movement sequences and coordinating both sides of his body.
  • Tires more quickly than expected during sustained physical activity.

His ability to follow simple movement instructions is a solid base for building more complex motor skills through structured practice.

Section 10

Fine Motor Skills

Ali's fine motor skills are emerging, though reduced hand strength and attention limit his precision and stamina.

  • Follows 1–2 step fine motor instructions ("put the block in the box, then close it") with support.
  • His grasp during drawing and manipulation tasks is still immature for his age.
  • His grip tires quickly during table-top tasks.
  • Doesn't yet use both hands together consistently during building play.
Section 11

Balance and Postural Control

Ali's balance and postural control are reduced, in line with the vestibular and proprioceptive difficulties noted earlier.

  • Weak balance when standing on one leg, moving quickly, or changing position.
  • Becomes unsteady when reaching beyond his base of support.
  • Uneven or unstable surfaces are especially difficult for him.
  • Widens his stance to compensate while standing and walking.
Section 12

Upper Limb Function

Reduced strength affects Ali's arms during weight-bearing, reaching, and hands-on tasks.

  • Reduced strength during weight-bearing activities (crawling, wheelbarrow walks, push-based play).
  • Tires during sustained reaching or overhead movements.
  • Grip and carrying stamina are below what's expected for his age.
  • Can use both arms together during play, but tires quickly.
Section 13

Attention and Participation

Ali's overall attention span is short, but his participation depends heavily on how motivating the activity is.

  • Struggles to sustain attention on non-preferred or structured tasks and often needs redirection.
  • Attention and participation increase markedly with motivating or sensory-rich activities.
  • Follows 1–2 step instructions across both gross and fine motor tasks.
  • Benefits from short tasks, movement breaks, and rewards he cares about.
Observed Strength Motivation makes a real difference for Ali. When an activity interests him, his engagement, cooperation, and ability to follow instructions all improve.
Section 14

Clinical Impression

Ali is a 3-year-2-month-old boy whose developmental profile is shaped mainly by sensory processing difficulties — across proprioceptive, vestibular, visual, tactile, and auditory input — along with reduced upper limb strength, weak balance, and a short attention span.

Together, these make it harder for him to stay engaged in structured tasks, refine his motor skills, and maintain social behaviours like eye contact. At the same time, Ali is active and motivated, and he can follow 1–2 step instructions and participate well when a task engages him — real strengths to build on.

This mix of challenges and strengths points to a good outlook with a structured, sensory-informed, motivation-driven therapy program.

Section 15

Summary of Findings

In summary, this assessment identified the following:

  • Weak, inconsistent eye contact.
  • Sensory processing difficulties across the proprioceptive, vestibular, visual, tactile, and auditory systems.
  • Reduced upper limb strength affecting weight-bearing and hands-on tasks.
  • Weak balance and postural control.
  • A short attention span, especially for non-preferred tasks.
  • Ability to follow 1–2 step gross and fine motor instructions.
  • Good participation when activities are motivating.

Overall, Ali is a child with clear areas of need and genuine strengths in motivation and instruction-following — a solid foundation for consistent therapy support.

Section 17

Therapy Recommendations

We recommend Occupational Therapy 2–3 times a week, focusing on:

  • Sensory integration work covering proprioceptive, vestibular, visual, tactile, and auditory regulation.
  • Arm strengthening through weight-bearing and resistance-based play.
  • Balance and postural control practice.
  • Gross and fine motor skill-building, using his ability to follow 1–2 step instructions.
  • Attention-building through motivating, sensory-rich activities.
  • Structured social play to encourage eye contact and shared engagement.
Section 18

Home Program Recommendations for Parents

  • Daily "heavy work" activities: swinging, trampoline jumping, deep-pressure hugs, and joint compression.
  • Short, low-pressure tactile play with sand, rice bins, playdough, or textured toys to build tolerance.
  • Balance activities: walking a low beam, animal walks, or a simple obstacle course.
  • Arm-strengthening games: wheelbarrow walks, climbing, pushing and pulling.
  • Short, clear 1–2 step instructions paired with gestures to help him succeed.
  • Keep structured tasks brief, and lean on activities he enjoys to build attention.
  • Encourage eye contact through fun interactive games (peekaboo, songs, taking turns) rather than asking directly.
  • Keep routines consistent between home and therapy.
Section 19

School / Nursery Recommendations (if applicable)

  • Regular movement breaks between structured activities.
  • Simple 1–2 step instructions paired with visual or gestural cues.
  • Sensory-friendly options where available, such as a quiet corner, therapy putty, or a weighted lap pad.
  • Play-based, motivating learning activities.
  • A seat in a low-distraction area to support attention.
  • Staff awareness of his sensory sensitivities and reduced balance, especially during group or outdoor play.
  • Regular communication between nursery staff, caregivers, and the therapy team.
Section 20

Prognosis

With consistent Occupational Therapy, a structured sensory-motor program, and active support at home, Ali is expected to make steady, gradual progress in sensory regulation, upper limb strength, balance, attention, and social engagement. Progress will depend on consistent strategies across home and therapy, with regular check-ins to adjust his goals as he grows.
